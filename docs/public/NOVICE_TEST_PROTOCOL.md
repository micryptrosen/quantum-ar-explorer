# First-time learner observation protocol

This is a protocol for a future owner-administered session. It is not evidence
that a novice study, physical SPECS test or external endorsement has occurred.

Record the exact source commit/build, input route, preview environment or actual
device, and whether the participant has studied quantum computing before.
Use an anonymous session code; names and contact details are unnecessary.
Keep human mouse, physical pinch, CLAD coordinate selection and LEAF results
separate. Automated interactions do not establish human usability.

## Twenty-second discovery observation

Use a first-time participant who has not studied quantum computing. Give no
explanation of phase beforehand. Show the optional discovery or its genuine
recorded opening once, for approximately twenty seconds. Record the actual
duration, starting state, any assistance, and whether they watched or interacted.
Ask these neutral questions before showing the technical Details:

1. What were the original odds?
2. What changed between the experiments?
3. Why did applying the same final operation produce different results?
4. What does that tell you about quantum information?

Record the participant's exact explanation, including uncertainty or errors.
Do not imply that the intermediate 50/50 states already predict certain different
results. The final operation changes their probabilities; measurement then
confirms those computed predictions. Understanding that specific relationship
is the target, rather than mastery of quantum theory. Do not score vocabulary
such as "Hadamard" as necessary understanding.

Keep three kinds of evidence separate: technical verification of the model and
preview, heuristic design review, and actual human novice observation. If nobody
participates, record **human novice comprehension: not tested**. Do not substitute
an automated review, scripted scenario, or a developer's explanation for a novice.

## Complete beginner journey

Ask the learner to think aloud. Say: "We are testing the experience. Please tell
me what you expect, what you notice, and anything that is unclear." Do not teach
the answer or point at a control before they try. Record any assistance given.

1. Open the beginner lesson. Ask what the experience is about and what they
   would do first. Observe the first action without directing it.
2. Ask them to change the classical bit, then explain what a bit can be.
3. Ask them to prepare a qubit with equal measurement probabilities. Ask what
   they predict will happen on the next measurement and why.
4. Ask them to measure, explain the result and measure again. Then ask them to
   start a fresh experiment and describe how that differs from repeating.
5. Ask them to complete the challenge, find the optional laboratory and return
   to their lesson. Observe whether progress and results remain understandable.
6. If the tested build includes phase comparison, ask them to explore it and
   explain why A and B can have the same immediate probabilities yet different
   results after the final H. Record their explanation before offering one.
7. Ask them to find help, turn motion off, reposition/recenter and restart.
   Record missing features as unavailable rather than coaching around them.

For each task record completion, wrong turns, assistance, recovery and the
learner's explanation in their own words. Measure elapsed time only if someone
actually times it; distinguish task time from loading and setup. Note label
readability, control discovery and any reported visual discomfort in the tested
context. Desktop findings do not establish physical additive-display contrast,
wearer comfort or hand-tracking performance.

Finish with: "What was clearest? What was confusing? What do you think changed
when you measured?" Record individual observations and proposed corrections.
Do not turn a small convenience sample into a general learning-effect claim.
